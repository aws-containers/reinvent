Kubernetes node protection · Companion to the troubleshooting dashboard

Can the node still run its pods?

A node can be 100% busy and perfectly healthy. It can also report Ready while quietly failing the work only it can do. This guide shows the difference one layer at a time, and where each layer appears on the dashboard.

01 · The node's own work

Before a pod runs, the node has to do work of its own.

The scheduler has picked a node. Nothing is running yet. Step through what has to happen next, and notice who does each piece: not the pod, the node.

Go deeper: the first 5 minutes, the 15-minute settling rule, and the rest of section 1

First 5 minutes. A brand-new node's kubelet asks for sandboxes before its networking is ready, and those calls fail in every healthy scale-up (108 of 109 errors in a healthy 50K-pod run). The dashboard counts them in the blue-grey Errors while nodes boot tile and does not call them harm. Runtime refused work counts nodes, not errors, and applies the source guide's paging rule: errors in two 5-minute windows, or on three or more nodes at once. A single error that does not repeat sits in the risk row as Runtime errors, isolated (a healthy 50K run had one).

Settled nodes. Pod start and the waiting tiles only judge nodes that have reported for 15 minutes (25 minutes for the 10-minute sustained readings). Young nodes start pods slowly while they pull images and build their first Service map; that is capacity, not protection.

Section 1 also holds: Kubelet's pod-state check age, oldest nodes (over 60 s its view of its containers is stale), Kubelets not answering, Node software restarted (a new harm signal: the kubelet or containerd restarted after the node's first 5 minutes, so it crashed or was killed for memory; the kill itself is not in the metrics, the node's journal names it) and Container stop time, slowest nodes.

Moved to the Engineer's detail tab (section 3): Runtime call time, mean, by kind (a mean on a log scale: one very slow call among many reads small), Pod-state check duration, slowest nodes (how long the PLEG pass itself took; coarse buckets make the lines two-humped) and Pod start time, all pods (a heatmap: a healthy fleet is one bright band near the bottom). They have no harm line of their own; read them after section 1 has named a node and a time.

02 · Busy is not waiting

A CPU at 100% tells you nothing about who got their turn.

Here is one CPU over ten seconds. The pods are allowed to fill it. The question is what happens when the kubelet has something to do.

Scene · one CPU, ten seconds · the kubelet wakes up four times
CPU use100%same in every scenario
kubelet CPU waiting0%share of time with work but no CPU
Full stall0%share of time nobody on the node moved
What the pods seefinestarts and stops on time

The pods use every free cycle. Each time the kubelet wakes, a CPU is handed to it within microseconds: hatched waiting is almost zero. CPU use reads 100% and nothing is wrong.

CPU use

"How much of the time were the CPUs executing?" High is allowed here: the pods may fill theirs. It cannot say who was delayed.

CPU waiting (pressure)

"What share of the time did this group have work ready and no CPU?" This names the delayed. 2% means 2% of the window spent waiting, not 2% fewer cores.

Full stall

"What share of the time was every task in the group stuck?" On memory or disk. The group made no progress at all, whoever owned the CPUs.

CPU use measures execution. CPU waiting names who was delayed. Full stall means nobody moved.Section 2 of the dashboard is built on the second and third, and treats the first as context only.

Go deeper: where these numbers come from, and the 10-minute rule

Pressure Stall Information (PSI) is the kernel's own accounting, per cgroup and for the whole node. "Some" pressure is the share of time at least one task waited; "full" is the share when every task waited. The dashboard reads CPU "some" for the kubelet's and containerd's cgroups and for each agent pod, and memory and I/O "full" for the hosts and the node.

Held for 10 minutes. The tiles and the table take the lowest 5-minute reading inside a 10-minute window, so one burst does not count. The lines are the source guide's: 2% CPU waiting, 5% full stall. Averaged over whole runs at 75K to 200K pods, the average node's kubelet waited 0.35 to 0.47% and its containerd 0.64 to 0.87%.

Node software stalled on memory (also in section 2, and a tile in the risk row) is the full-stall share for the kubelet's and containerd's cgroups, with the same 5% line held 10 minutes; the whole node stalling the same way is the harm tile Node stalled on memory; chapter 04 shows why it matters. Whole-node run queue wait per CPU sums every task's waiting on the node and divides by CPU count. It can exceed 1 because several tasks wait at once. It says the CPUs are contended, not whose wait mattered.

03 · The reserved lane

Four CPUs the pods never touch.

Each protected node keeps 4 of its 32 vCPUs for its own software: the kubelet, containerd, the shims that supervise containers, and a short list of agents. The pods run on the other 28. That sounds like one setting. It is four agreements.

Scene · a 32-vCPU node · which CPUs are the lane, and who is allowed on them

Capacity: subtracting 4 from allocatable only changes scheduling arithmetic. The scheduler stops placing 4 vCPUs' worth of requests here. It does not move a single process.

An idle lane is doing its job

Node work arrives in bursts, when many pods start or stop together. The lane needs room for those bursts. In a healthy 50K run the median node's lane ran under 10% busy; that is not waste.

A CPU limit is a different wait

An agent can sit on the right CPUs and still be stopped by its own CPU limit (throttling). More reserved CPUs do not help it; a higher limit does. The dashboard keeps the two apart.

A reservation is four agreements: capacity, topology, placement, and the agent exception.The dashboard proves the two it can see from outside. The node's own per-minute check proves the rest and writes the result to its journal.

illustrative
Go deeper: the "CPU split misconfigured" tile, and what metrics cannot prove

CPU split misconfigured (a tile in the Harm row) compares two numbers on every node with a static CPU manager: the CPUs the scheduler was told are reserved (capacity minus allocatable) and the CPUs the kubelet actually keeps pods off (capacity minus its shared pool and any whole CPUs it gave a pod). A difference means accounting and placement describe different boundaries. The guidance is blunt: replace the node; an incomplete node makes every measurement on it uninterpretable.

What metrics cannot prove. Which physical CPUs are in the lane, whether they are whole cores, whether systemd's and the plugin's placement took effect. Those need the live cgroup state, which the node's per-minute check reads. The Configuration tab's Failed contract checks tile is wired for a published version of that check; in the lab the check writes to the journal instead, so the tile reads "not exported" in grey. Grey there means unverified by metrics, never passing.

04 · The memory floor

Owning a CPU does not keep your code in memory.

Reserved CPUs reserve nothing in RAM. When the pods fill memory, the kernel takes pages back from everyone, including the kubelet's and containerd's own code and the image files every pod runs from. Push the slider and watch.

Heartbeat age10 sseen from the API server
Nodeworkingpods start and stop
Podsall runningno OOM kills

Plenty of room. The host's code and containerd's image pages sit in memory untouched.

A floor is not a ceiling

memory.min protects the memory a group holds beneath the line from reclaim. It allocates nothing in advance and caps nothing: use may go over the floor, and that is allowed.

The workload pays for it

With the host's pages off limits, memory runs out sooner elsewhere. A pod is OOM-killed while the node keeps answering. That is a trade, not a free fix: the pod was still lost.

kubelet 775 MiB+containerd 3,774 MiB=runtime.slice 4,549 MiBconfigured in the protected profile at 170 pods and 2,304 MiB of unpacked images; another class needs its own sum

The sign of a missing floor is the host reading its own code back from disk during memory pressure.Without one, the lab's kubelet read back 888 to 4,453 pages a second and nodes went not Ready. With one, 1 to 11 pages a second and the node stayed up.

illustrative
Go deeper: reclaim, OOM kills and the late MemoryPressure condition

Node-wide reclaim shows pages scanned by tasks freeing memory for themselves and file pages read back soon after eviction. It says the node is short of memory. It does not say whose pages were taken; the code-read-back panel names the victim.

Out-of-memory kills and memory pressure. The node-wide kill counter (and the Out-of-memory kills, all tile) does not identify the victim. On a floored node a pod kill under pressure is consistent with the floor working. A kill of the kubelet or containerd is harm, but the kernel charges that kill to the node as a whole, so the metrics cannot name the victim. The dashboard reads the consequence instead: the Node software restarted harm tile and its section 1 panel count restarts after the node's first 5 minutes; the node's journal says whether it was a crash or a kill. Kills arriving with a node that stopped checking in are harm too. MemoryPressure is a late condition; do not wait for it before reading the stall panels.

The floor does not stop every stall. Floored kubelets still stalled 13 to 22% while the pods sat at their memory ceiling, on nodes that never went not Ready. What the floor buys is that their pages stay, so the stall ends instead of becoming a freeze.

05 · The teardown path

Stopping a container is work too, and it can wait on the disk.

A stop looks like one event. It is two: the application shutting down on its own schedule, then the runtime cleaning up after it. Only the second is the node's problem, and the kernel can leave it sleeping on the disk.

Scene · one container stop, second by second · toggle the disk
Container stop time2.3 swhat the kubelet measures
Of which, the pod's own1.5 sapplication shutdown
Of which, the node's0.8 sruntime cleanup
Node disk stall0%every task waiting on I/O

The application exits inside its grace period; the runtime removes the task, unmounts the writable layer and removes the sandbox in under a second. Under a CPU-heavy tenant, protected nodes stopped containers in about 2.3 s where unprotected ones took 10 to 92 s.

Separate the pod's own shutdown from the runtime's cleanup. A plateau at 30 s is a pod using its whole grace period; a stop that keeps growing with disk busy time is the node.Reserved CPUs do not finish disk I/O. A process can own its CPU and still sleep in the kernel.

Section 1 for the outcome, Section 3 · Disk tab for the cause

Stuck tasksDisk

Read it: the kernel found a task stuck in uninterruptible sleep past its timeout (120 s by default). The journal names the task only for the first 10 after boot.

illustrative
Go deeper: volatile overlays, the Disk tab's other panels and the journal

Writable layers are mounted volatile on protected nodes, so stopping a container no longer waits for a sync of the whole filesystem. A saturated root volume can still slow everything that touches it: image pulls, container starts, and the unmount itself.

Also on the Disk tab: Teardown calls, 9 in 10 under (stop and remove, 90th percentile; stops that grow with disk busy time are teardown waiting on the disk) and Root filesystem used (full disks fail pulls and starts; the kubelet evicts pods near the end).

The strongest proof lives in the journal. The runtime's own deadline errors at teardown are log lines, not metrics. The source guide's chapter 11 gives a Logs Insights query: three or more distinct containers on one node in five minutes, sustained into the next window. Run it for the node and window you found on the dashboard.

06 · Three network jobs

Three different jobs hide behind "the network is slow".

A pod that cannot get an address, a Service whose route is not programmed yet, and a packet dropped after it reached the node are three failures with three owners. Only one of them has anything to do with CPU.

Scene · pick the job · follow it to the step that can fail

The kubelet asks containerd for a sandbox; containerd asks the CNI; aws-node must hand over a free address from the pool it has attached. When the pool is empty, run_podsandbox fails on a node that is otherwise perfectly prompt.

Name the failed network job before touching a network setting.Reserved CPUs cannot mint addresses, apply Service changes faster than they arrive, or raise an instance's packet allowance.

illustrative
Go deeper: receive steering, and what host drop counters cannot see

Receive Packet Steering (RPS) spreads receive processing across CPUs. The validated profile points it at the workload CPUs so the lane stays quiet, and keeps Receive Flow Steering off (it was counterproductive under saturated backlogs). Turn RPS on only with measured drops that line up with one hot CPU, and only if the instance allowances stay quiet. Spreading cost 20 to 40% more receive CPU at moderate rates.

Zero host drops does not clear the application. The UDP buffer counter sees only host-network sockets. A pod's own socket drops happen inside its namespace, and the lab does not keep cAdvisor's pod-interface counter. The last hop needs application evidence.

Also on the tab: Service changes waiting to be applied (pending work should drain; full update time stays bounded in nftables mode) and CPU spent receiving packets (compare with peers, and before and after any steering change).

07 · Trust the evidence

A frozen node stops telling you it is frozen.

Most of this dashboard is the node describing itself. The moment it matters most, that description can stop. The dashboard reads the node from two sides so you can tell a quiet node from a silent one.

Scene · one node over forty minutes · inside view and outside view

Top: the node's own metrics (scraped from the kubelet, cAdvisor and node-exporter). Bottom: its lease, renewed every 10 s and read from the API server. When the node freezes in memory reclaim, the top goes blank and the bottom climbs. Blank and zero look alike on a chart; they mean opposite things.

"–" · no reading

The node had not settled long enough for that check, or there was nothing to measure: no sandbox was created, no pod started. Nothing happened.

"no data" · no observation

The source sent nothing. Every tile reads grey "no data", never 0, when its own source was silent. Grey is unknown, not healthy.

"not exported" · not measured here

The metric exists in the design but not in this cluster (the contract check writes to the journal). Protection is unverified by metrics, not passing.

Read from both sides: the node's own metrics, and the API server's view of its heartbeat. On this dashboard grey always means unknown.Two tiles exist only for this: Kubelet went silent (inside view gone, in the Harm row) and Nodes with missing telemetry (a source never answered in the range, in the At risk row).

Go deeper: sampling, thresholds, and what the lines are not

Sampling. Heartbeat age is read every 30 s, so a reading can fall up to 30 s short of a real gap: the 60 s line reliably catches gaps of about 90 s and longer. Counters are read step by step so a jump after a scrape gap of up to an hour still counts once; treat totals as estimates, not audits.

Worst-node lines can switch nodes at every timestamp, and a node leaving a top-8 list has not necessarily recovered. Before asserting that two things lined up on one node, select that node so every panel shows only it.

Thresholds are starting points from 32-vCPU nodes at 170 pods. The source guide's lines are 2% CPU waiting and 5% full stall held 10 minutes, 60 s pod-state age, 30 s heartbeat, 90 s Service map, a 5 s pod-start objective, and a page on runtime errors in two 5-minute windows or on several nodes. Every other line (15/30 s pod start, 35 s stop, 60 s heartbeat, 80% lane, 100/800 pages a second, and orange at one or two nodes, red from three on the harm tiles) is the dashboard's, set from the lab's runs. A panel's tooltip says which it uses.

Do not page on CPU
